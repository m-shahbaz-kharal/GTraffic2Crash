Steps 03–04

The data, and what it looks like

The map is given to the network as constant input channels. It is free information that would otherwise cost capacity to rediscover: 28 % of cells touch road and hold about 80 % of all vehicle mass.

Half the town is standing still

This is the number that says whether the task is worth doing at all.

Vehicles essentially stationary (< 0.1 cell per step)55 %
Vehicles moving more than half a cell per step37 %
Fleet moving at any instant45 %, swinging 32–57 %
Median displacement of a moving vehicle over the 5 s horizon9.0 cells · 36 m

Half the town is stopped at any moment, which is what urban traffic with signals looks like, and is why persistence is a strong baseline. It is also what makes the problem interesting rather than degenerate: the model's real job is to tell the vehicle that will sit still for five seconds from the one that will travel thirty-six metres — a distinction that cannot be made from a single frame. It needs the queue, the signal phase implied by the traffic's own behaviour, and the road ahead.

Step 05

The network

Two measured results from the literature drove the shape, not taste.

Attention along one axis at a time

Earthformer ablates seven ways of factorising attention over a space-time grid under identical conditions. Attending to time, then height, then width — plus a handful of global vectors to restore long-range mixing — wins on every metric on both benchmarks and is among the cheapest. Joint space-time cuboids and Video-Swin windows are strictly dominated.

No recurrence

On low-frequency grid data — traffic flow and weather, the closest public analogues — the OpenSTL benchmark finds that predicting every future frame in one parallel pass matches recurrent rollout at a twenty-sixth of the compute, and sidesteps autoregressive drift entirely. Town traffic at 2 Hz is low-frequency.
